Our project asks a simple question.
Can a rule that checks one price at a time protect customers over many rounds?

SLIDE 2 - How the game works

There are two shops.
Each shop wants to make a profit.
Customers can buy from either shop, or buy nothing.

We use a rule called a guard.
It allows prices that give nearly the best profit against the other shop's last price.

We also use a cap. It limits both shops' prices for that round.
People can choose prices in our game, or watch saved results.
The game shows the rules. It is not a study of human players.

SLIDE 3 - More price choices

We calculate the lowest customer-benefit score the rule allows over many rounds.
A higher score is better for customers.

With fifteen price choices, this score is about seventy-two point one.
When prices can change by any small amount within the same price range, it falls to about sixty-six point three.
Our target stays at sixty-eight point five.

So the same starting cap does not always protect our target.

SLIDE 4 - Keeping the guard

Next, we test programs that learn to choose prices.
Both groups start with the same cap and train for two million rounds.
They have the same clock and memory size.

One group keeps the guard.
The other can then use all prices on the original price board.

All twelve bars are below zero.
Keeping the guard helps customers in all twelve pairs.
The average gain is about twelve point seven score units.
More price choices also change the learning task.

SLIDE 5 - Using the cap again

We also test using the cap again every four rounds.
Both groups have the same clock and memory size.

All twelve bars are above zero.
Using the cap again helps customers by about zero point nine six units on average.

Results still change between one million and two million training rounds.
So we cannot say the learning has fully settled.

SLIDE 6 - How we checked the work

The shops learn from their profit, using a method called Q-learning.
We did not use language models as players.

After training, we stop learning and watch the saved price patterns repeat.

A second program checks every training round in these two studies.
That is forty-eight million rounds in one study, and twenty-four million in the other.

The programs share some software.
So the check could still miss a shared mistake.

SLIDE 7 - What we learned

The guard helps customers in these tests.
But it does not guarantee our target for every allowed choice.

We tested one simple market with a fixed training time.
This does not prove the same result holds in real markets.
We cannot claim that the shops have formed a lasting price agreement.

Our report explains the methods, results and limits.
It also explains how AI tools helped us build, check and write this work.
